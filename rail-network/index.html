Building a theoretical high-speed rail network with the TRAC Framework.

This page tells the story of an experimental workflow for generating conceptual high-speed rail networks from nodes, corridors, hubs, feeder trees, gap-fill links, and iterative evaluation.

The maps are exploratory models. They are not final investment proposals, engineering alignments, or costed plans.

Why this project exists

From network sketch to decision-support workflow.

A national rail map can look persuasive even when the logic behind it is unclear. This project takes the opposite approach. It treats the map as an output of an explicit workflow: identify nodes, select hubs, build a backbone, connect regional destinations, fill missing links, and evaluate where the system still performs poorly.

The goal is not to produce a single perfect rail plan. The goal is to create a repeatable way to compare design choices. Each method changes one part of the workflow so we can see how the network responds.

What the maps show

Conceptual high-speed rail networks based on hubs, corridors, feeder trees, and gap-fill links.

What the maps do not show

Detailed engineering feasibility, exact station siting, construction costs, land acquisition, or environmental permitting.

TRAC Framework

Theoretical Rail Alignment & Connectivity Framework.

The TRAC Framework is a conceptual method for generating and evaluating theoretical rail networks. It starts with places that should be served, turns some of them into hubs, connects those hubs through a backbone, and then adds feeder trees, fallback spokes, and gap-fill links.

01

Identify nodes

Use population, visitation, universities, growth, strategic borders, and other context-specific indicators.

02

Select hubs

Choose the cities or transfer points that should anchor the network structure.

03

Build the backbone

Connect major hubs through algorithmic routing and manually reviewed strategic corridors.

04

Add regional access

Use feeder trees first, with fallback spokes only where the feeder tree creates poor access.

05

Fill gaps

Add ordinary augmenting links where the network creates large detours between nearby places.

06

Evaluate and revise

Use air-corridor checks, visual review, and existing rail comparison to identify weak parts of the network.

Method 1

Fixed-hub hub-and-spoke with sparse gap fill.

Method 1 is the baseline. It starts with a small set of fixed hubs, connects them through a strategic backbone, and routes terminals to their nearest hub as direct spokes. A sparse gap-fill step adds links where the initial graph creates large detours between nearby destinations.

What to notice

The structure is transparent, but dense regions can produce competing radial spokes.

Why it matters

This baseline makes it easier to see what changes when feeder trees are introduced in Method 2.

Evaluation

Air-corridor checks are used as an iteration tool.

The air-corridor checks compare high-frequency air corridors with the rail paths produced by each method. The score is based on the rail-network distance relative to straight-line distance. A lower detour ratio means the generated rail graph follows the air corridor more directly.

Method 3 is not repeated here because its flight-corridor result was effectively duplicated by the Method 2 comparison. Method 4 is shown because it changes the hub-selection logic and adds an MST-based population hub backbone.

Method 2

Hierarchical backbone–feeder construction.

Method 2 keeps the national hub backbone but changes the regional access layer. Instead of drawing every local connection as a direct spoke to a hub, it uses feeder trees where several destinations can share a corridor. Direct fallback spokes remain only where a feeder tree would create excessive detours.

This version also adds Phoenix as a Southwest hub and treats Denver–Phoenix as a backbone corridor routed through Colorado Springs, Pueblo, Trinidad, Santa Fe, Albuquerque, and Flagstaff.

Method 3

North American manually guided backbone–feeder construction.

Method 3 expands the framework beyond the contiguous United States. It adds a deliberately simple Canadian west–east trunk from Vancouver through Calgary, Winnipeg, Toronto, Montreal, and Quebec City, plus selected north–south cross-border corridors.

It also removes the corridor-access-anchor experiment, so feeder trees and fallback spokes connect to fixed hubs rather than arbitrary intermediate backbone points. This makes the map more controlled and easier to interpret.

Manual cleanup

Boise–Portland is retained as an ordinary gap-fill. Miami/Miami Beach–Naples and Omaha–Kansas City are retained, while redundant Fort Lauderdale–Naples and Des Moines–Kansas City branches are removed.

What remains experimental

Some regions still depend on manual review because local fallback and gap-fill links can become messy in dense networks.

Method 4

Population-based hub-tree backbone–feeder construction.

Method 4 is designed to be more repeatable. The U.S. hub set is selected by a population rule: the largest U.S. cities are treated as fixed hubs, and the largest city in each otherwise uncovered state is added so that every state has at least one population anchor. The remaining large cities stay in the model as ordinary nodes rather than hubs.

After hub selection, Method 4 builds an algorithmic minimum-spanning-tree backbone across all fixed hubs using straight-line distance. This gives every population and state-coverage hub at least one backbone connection before feeder trees, fallback spokes, or gap-fill links are generated.

The MST is not treated as the final rail plan. Manual strategic corridors are layered on top where geography and corridor logic matter, such as the West Coast spine, Chicago–DC via Columbus, Pittsburgh, Morgantown, Cumberland, and Martinsburg, Canada cross-border routes, and selected Midwest and Southwest links. A small number of duplicate MST links are pruned where they conflict with stronger manually guided corridors.

Why this method is more repeatable

Hub selection follows a population rule first, rather than starting from a fully manual hub list.

Why manual links still matter

A tree guarantees connectivity, but strategic corridors are still needed to represent readable regional rail structure.

What we learned

The maps separate hub choice, feeder logic, geographic expansion, and repeatability.

Feeder trees reduce radial clutter.

Method 2 shows why regional feeder trees are useful. They make local access look more like a rail corridor and less like many independent spokes to a single hub.

Manual corridors are still needed.

Method 3 and Method 4 show that algorithmic routing alone can miss the way people expect regions to connect. Strategic corridors improve legibility.

Population hubs improve repeatability.

Method 4 makes the hub set less arbitrary, but a population-based hub set still needs pruning and corridor review to avoid awkward local structures.

Next steps

What we want to do next.

Improve feeder-tree rules

Test different detour thresholds, catchment definitions, and candidate-edge rules to avoid over-cleaning or under-cleaning local networks.

Add stronger demand data

Incorporate population, travel demand, current rail ridership, airport flows, and regional development priorities.

Model constraints and costs

Introduce terrain, protected areas, urban constraints, construction cost proxies, and capacity assumptions.

Compare alternatives

Generate multiple networks under different objectives, such as coverage, emissions reduction, travel time, or equity.
