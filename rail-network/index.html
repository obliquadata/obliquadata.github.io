Preliminary research note

Building a passenger rail network from first principles.

This project tests a generalizable framework for designing and evaluating a national passenger rail network when the goal is not simply to map what already exists, but to ask what a coherent rail system could look like if planned around hubs, corridors, geographic constraints, and travel demand.

Project idea

From network sketch to decision-support workflow.

The aim is to develop a repeatable method for rail-network design that can be adapted to other countries. Instead of treating rail planning as only a route-drawing exercise, the framework links spatial data preparation, hub selection, network construction, and corridor evaluation into one transparent workflow.

Why compare methods?

A pure hub-and-spoke system is simple and easy to explain, but it can create unrealistic radial clutter around dense regions. Comparing methods makes it easier to see where the framework needs more hierarchy, local feeder logic, and demand-responsive correction.

Status

These are preliminary outputs. The maps are not a final investment proposal, engineering alignment, or costed plan. They are exploratory models to support comparison, critique, and further development.

Framework under development

A general workflow for constructing rail networks from scratch.

Method 1: fixed-hub hub-and-spoke with sparse gap fill

The first prototype starts with fixed national hubs, connects them through a manually reviewed strategic backbone, and then routes important terminals directly to their nearest hub. A sparse gap-fill step adds links where the resulting network creates large detours between nearby terminals.

Method 2: hierarchical backbone–feeder construction

The second method keeps the same broad backbone idea but modifies the spoke layer. In dense hub catchments, selected direct spokes are replaced by constrained regional feeder trees. Direct fallback spokes are restored where a feeder tree would create an excessive detour to the hub.

Version 3: Phoenix hub refinement

The next refinement uses the flight-corridor evaluation to change the network structure. Phoenix is added as a Southwest hub, Los Angeles–San Antonio is split through Phoenix, and Denver–Phoenix becomes a backbone corridor rather than a post-hoc priority link.

Method 3: North American manually guided backbone

Method 3 removes the corridor-access-anchor experiment, returns feeder assignment to fixed hubs only, and expands the manually guided backbone into Canada with a west–east Canadian trunk and selected north–south cross-border corridors. This updated data also removes the redundant Fort Lauderdale–Naples and Des Moines–Kansas City gap-fill links while retaining Miami/Miami Beach–Naples, Omaha–Kansas City, and Boise–Portland.

Method 4: population-based hub selection

Method 4 tests a more repeatable hub-selection rule. Instead of relying mainly on manually chosen U.S. hubs, it selects the largest U.S. cities by population as fixed hubs, adds the largest city in each otherwise uncovered state, and keeps the remaining large cities as ordinary nodes. These hubs are then connected to nearby hubs where the geography produces a sensible corridor.

Method 1

Fixed-hub hub-and-spoke with sparse gap fill.

This initial version connects fixed hubs through a strategic backbone, routes terminals to their nearest hub as direct spokes, and adds augmenting links where the network produces large detours. It is useful as a transparent baseline, but it can produce competing radial spokes in dense regions and can miss some important city-pair corridors, such as the later Denver–Phoenix priority connection.

Method 2

Hierarchical backbone–feeder construction.

This newer version keeps the national hub backbone but replaces suitable direct-spoke clusters with regional feeder trees. The result should be easier to interpret as a rail system: major corridors remain visible, local access becomes more corridor-like, and fallback spokes only remain where the feeder tree would create excessive detours. It also adds Phoenix as a fixed Southwest hub and treats Denver–Phoenix as a backbone corridor, routed through Colorado Springs, Pueblo, Trinidad, Santa Fe, Albuquerque, and Flagstaff.

Method 3

North American manually guided backbone–feeder construction.

Method 3 replaces the corridor-access-anchor experiment with a more controlled fixed-hub structure. Feeder trees and fallback spokes now connect only to fixed hubs, while the backbone expands from the United States into Canada. The Canadian layer uses one west–east trunk from Vancouver through Calgary, Winnipeg, Toronto, Montreal, and Quebec City, plus selected north–south cross-border corridors. The latest map keeps Boise–Portland as an ordinary gap-fill link, retains Miami/Miami Beach–Naples and Omaha–Kansas City, and removes redundant Fort Lauderdale–Naples and Des Moines–Kansas City gap-fill branches.

Method 4

Population-based hub backbone–feeder construction.

Method 4 recreates the North American backbone–feeder network with a more repeatable hub-selection rule. The U.S. hub set is based on population: the largest U.S. cities are treated as fixed hubs, and the largest city in each otherwise uncovered state is added so every state has at least one population anchor. The remaining cities from the largest-120 list stay in the model as ordinary nodes rather than hubs. Canadian hubs are kept from Method 3 to preserve the simple west–east trunk from Vancouver to Quebec City and the selected north–south cross-border corridors.

After the population hubs are selected, they are connected where the geography supports a coherent corridor. This includes nearest-neighbour style links where adjacent hubs form a sensible regional chain, plus manually reviewed structural corridors in the West, Midwest, South, Northeast, and Canada. The goal is to test whether a rule-based hub set can make the method more reproducible while still allowing geographic judgement where a pure nearest-neighbour graph would create awkward endpoints or missing trunk connections.

How to read the comparison

The four maps separate hub choice, feeder logic, geographic expansion, and repeatability.

Method 1 is the transparent baseline: fixed hubs, a strategic backbone, direct spokes, and sparse gap filling. Method 2 changes the regional access layer by introducing feeder trees and fallback spokes. Method 3 keeps the manually guided backbone idea, removes arbitrary corridor-access anchors, extends the network into Canada, and manually removes a small number of redundant gap-fill branches. Method 4 keeps the same broad North American structure but changes the U.S. hub-selection rule, using population-based hubs and state coverage to make the method easier to repeat.

Feeder trees vs. fallback spokes

Feeder trees are shared branches; fallback spokes are safety valves.

Regional feeder trees are shared local branches that connect nearby terminals through a corridor-like structure before linking them back to the hub. They reduce the unrealistic radial clutter that appears when every terminal receives its own separate spoke.

Fallback spokes are direct terminal-to-hub links that are restored after the feeder-tree step if the tree would make a terminal travel too far out of the way. They prevent the algorithm from over-cleaning the map at the expense of useful access.

What changed from Method 2 to Method 3

Method 3 adds Canada and tightens the access logic.

The earlier Method 3 experiment allowed spokes and feeder trees to connect to intermediate backbone waypoints. That made some areas cleaner, but it also created strange local attachments. The updated Method 3 removes those corridor access anchors, so feeder trees and fallback spokes attach only to fixed hubs. It adds Albuquerque as a Southwest interchange hub, Pittsburgh as an Appalachian/Great Lakes interchange hub, Boston as a New England hub, and Baltimore and Philadelphia along the DC–New York corridor; leaves Salt Lake City–Denver to the road-routing algorithm; adds a Canadian trunk from Vancouver to Quebec City with selected cross-border corridors; and adds Boise–Portland as a normal gap-fill link. This data refresh also removes redundant Fort Lauderdale–Naples and Des Moines–Kansas City gap-fill branches while retaining the cleaner Miami/Miami Beach–Naples and Omaha–Kansas City connections.

What changed from Method 3 to Method 4

Method 4 makes hub selection more rule-based.

Method 4 replaces the manually selected U.S. hub set with a population-based rule. The largest U.S. cities become fixed hubs, and the largest city in each state without one of those hubs is added for state coverage. The remaining cities from the largest-120 list remain as ordinary nodes, so they can still be served by feeder trees, fallback spokes, or gap-fill links. The Canadian hubs stay the same as Method 3. Once the hubs are selected, the method connects nearby hubs where the geography creates a sensible corridor, then adds manually reviewed structural links to close obvious endpoints such as the West Coast spine, western cross-links, Midwest chains, and Northeast corridors.

Next steps

What we want to do next.

Improve feeder-tree rules

Test different detour thresholds, catchment definitions, and candidate-edge rules to avoid over-cleaning or under-cleaning local networks.

Add stronger demand data

Incorporate population, travel demand, current rail ridership, airport flows, and regional development priorities.

Model constraints and costs

Introduce terrain, protected areas, urban constraints, construction cost proxies, and capacity assumptions.

Compare alternatives

Generate multiple networks under different objectives, such as coverage, emissions reduction, travel time, or equity.
