Preliminary research note

Building a passenger rail network from first principles.

This project tests a generalizable framework for designing and evaluating a national passenger rail network when the goal is not simply to map what already exists, but to ask what a coherent rail system could look like if planned around hubs, corridors, geographic constraints, and travel demand.

The present version focuses on the contiguous United States as a test case. It combines a hub backbone, spoke connections, and augmenting links to produce a preliminary network for comparison against major domestic air corridors.

Project idea

From network sketch to decision-support workflow.

The aim is to develop a repeatable method for rail-network design that can be adapted to other countries. Instead of treating rail planning as only a route-drawing exercise, the framework links spatial data preparation, hub selection, network construction, and corridor evaluation into one transparent workflow.

Why it matters

Rail debates often jump straight to preferred lines. A structured framework makes it easier to ask which connections are strategic, which corridors are plausible substitutes for short-haul flights, and where the network still needs refinement.

Status

These are preliminary outputs. The map is not a final investment proposal, engineering alignment, or costed plan. It is an exploratory model to support comparison, critique, and further development.

Framework under development

A general workflow for constructing rail networks from scratch.

01

Frame the planning question

Define the territorial scope, policy objective, and evaluation criteria before drawing routes.

02

Assemble spatial evidence

Prepare hubs, boundaries, settlement patterns, existing corridors, constraints, and candidate alignments.

03

Select strategic hubs

Identify a manageable set of national or regional anchors to structure the high-level network.

04

Construct the backbone

Connect core hubs through manually reviewed and algorithmically supported trunk corridors.

05

Add spokes and augmenting links

Extend coverage to important intermediate areas and add links to reduce network detours.

06

Evaluate against demand proxies

Compare rail distances with straight-line distances and high-frequency flight corridors to assess substitution potential.

Preliminary findings

Most tested corridors are promising, but gaps remain.

In the first evaluation of 25 high-frequency flight corridors, 19 are classified as good, 5 as okay, and 1 as poor. The weighted average rail detour ratio is approximately 1.25, which suggests that many major air markets are at least geometrically compatible with the prototype network, while several western and mountain-region connections still need better routing logic.

Next steps

What we want to do next.

Improve the framework

Make the design rules more explicit so the workflow can be transferred to other national contexts.

Add stronger demand data

Incorporate population, travel demand, current rail ridership, airport flows, and regional development priorities.

Model constraints and costs

Introduce terrain, protected areas, urban constraints, construction cost proxies, and capacity assumptions.

Compare alternatives

Generate multiple networks under different objectives, such as coverage, emissions reduction, travel time, or equity.
